Water Damage Restoration in Downtown American Fork, UT

Older houses near Main Street hide their plumbing and finishes behind plaster and original trim. Here is what to do first when water gets in, and how drying works in a historic home.

Call (385) 988-9410

The city's planning records describe the Downtown area as the blocks between 100 North, 100 South, 100 East and 100 West. The American Fork Historic District sits in and around that core, and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1998 with 104 contributing buildings. Residences in the district were built between 1868 and 1940, according to the registration summary.

That age matters when a pipe, roof or appliance lets go. Older buildings often have layered repairs, original plaster or lath, and finishes that cannot simply be replaced with a stock product. We cannot tell what is inside a wall until a moisture meter reads it and, if needed, a section is opened.

How water damage looks different in an older home

  • Plaster and lath hold water longer than modern drywall and can stain, bulge or loosen from the wall.
  • Original hardwood and wide trim can cup or separate if they stay wet, so careful drying is worth attempting before any removal.
  • Several generations of plumbing repairs can mean mixed pipe types behind the same wall.
  • Old crawl areas and low cellars can collect water that has nowhere to drain.

A leak that starts upstairs may travel along joists before it shows. The stain you see on a ceiling is often not where the water entered. Our water damage repair page explains how we trace the wet path with meters before deciding what to open.

Safety in the first hour

Do these things first

  • ✓Stop the source if you can. Shut the main valve to the house, or the valve under the fixture.
  • ✓Keep power off in wet areas. Only use the panel if you can reach it without standing in water.
  • ✓Move people and pets out of rooms with sagging ceilings.
  • ✓Do not run a vacuum, fan or heater that has touched standing water.
  • ✓If the water came from a drain or toilet, treat it as sewage. Leave it alone and see sewage backup cleanup.

Photograph every wet surface before anything is moved.

Material that may need testing before it is opened

Materials in buildings of this age can include lead paint or asbestos-containing products in some cases. Whether a specific house has them is unknown until a sample is tested. Testing before demolition is standard practice, and we will say so before any wall or ceiling is opened.

How drying is planned

  1. Map the moisture

    Meters and a thermal camera show how far the water traveled, not only where it is visible.

  2. Extract standing water

    Pumps and extraction units remove what can be lifted before drying starts.

  3. Dry in place where possible

    Air movers and dehumidifiers pull moisture out of trim, floors and framing. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard guides the drying goals.

  4. Open only what must be opened

    Wet insulation or soaked plaster is the usual reason to cut. The goal is to protect original finishes where drying can work.

Insurance and records

Coverage for a sudden pipe failure, a slow leak and a flood can each be treated differently, so ask your insurer about the cause you have. Keep photos, receipts and notes. A historic house may also have special repair needs. Mention that when you call your insurer.
