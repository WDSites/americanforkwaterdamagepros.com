How to Tell If You Have Mold in Your American Fork, UT Home

A room-by-room inspection routine, plus when a moisture meter helps and when a lab test does not.

Call (385) 988-9410

You can tell you probably have mold by looking for three things: visible fuzzy or speckled growth, a persistent musty smell and a source of moisture nearby. Growth often hides, so the more reliable method is to search for the water first. If you find damp materials, leaks or condensation, mold is a likely follow-on.

This guide gives an inspection routine you can finish in an afternoon with a flashlight. It also covers when a moisture meter is worth the money, when a lab test is not, and what to do once you know.

Visual signs to look for

Mold appears as spots, patches or fuzzy texture in colors that include black, green, white, gray and orange. It may look like a smudge at first. Wipe a small spot with a damp cloth. Dirt lifts, while growth often smears or comes back within days.

Other signs include paint that bubbles or peels, drywall that crumbles or feels soft, wallpaper that lifts at the seam and ceiling stains with a brown edge. Discoloration that follows a pipe or an outside wall is a stronger lead than one random spot.

What a musty smell tells you

A damp, earthy odor that is stronger in one room, one closet or near one wall points to moisture trapped somewhere. Smell the area with the doors closed for a few hours, then move to the spot where it intensifies. That is where to search first.

Smell alone does not prove mold, and it cannot be measured by a nose. Treat it as a pointer, then check the building materials and find the water.

Check the ceiling below any bathroom, the wall behind the water heater and the area around the washing machine, as well. Any place where water, cold surfaces and still air meet is a candidate.

Using a moisture meter

A moisture meter measures how wet drywall, wood or concrete is. Pin meters read the material at the tips, and pinless meters scan a larger area without leaving holes. Take a reading on a wall you know is dry, then compare it with the suspect area.

A big gap between the two readings means water is present or was recently. Crews use meters to find the edge of a wet area, since moisture wicks above and beyond a visible stain. If you find a wet wall, drying it within 24 to 48 hours is the goal the EPA sets for preventing mold growth, and water mitigation is the service that dries the structure.

To test or not to test

The EPA says that if visible mold is present, sampling is usually unnecessary. The next steps are the same regardless of the species: stop the moisture, dry the area and remove the growth and damaged materials. The EPA also says there are no federal limits for mold in buildings, so a lab number has nothing federal to be measured against.

Testing can make sense when you have a persistent smell with no visible source, when you need documentation for a dispute or when a professional has a specific question to answer. Ask what the result will change before paying for it. Questions about symptoms belong to a physician, not a test kit.

A 20-minute home inspection

  • ✓Look for staining, fuzzy patches, bubbling paint and soft drywall
  • ✓Check under every sink with a flashlight
  • ✓Pull furniture away from exterior walls in bedrooms and the basement
  • ✓Look inside closets on outside walls
  • ✓Check the ceiling below bathrooms and under the stairs
  • ✓Peek into the attic near fan ducts and roof vents
  • ✓Note where the smell is strongest

What to do next

  1. Find the water

    A leak, condensation, roof entry or yard water is behind it. Without finding it, cleaning fails.

  2. Stop and dry

    Fix the source, run fans and a dehumidifier and keep wet items from spreading moisture.

  3. Decide how big it is

    The EPA notes that areas smaller than about 10 square feet can often be cleaned by the homeowner.

  4. Scrub or discard

    Scrub hard surfaces with detergent and water and dry completely, but porous materials such as carpet or ceiling tiles may have to be thrown away.

  5. Call if it is large or unclear

    Large areas, wall cavities or contaminated water call for a professional.

If the source was sewage or contaminated water, the EPA says to call a professional with experience before touching anything. When growth is larger than a small patch, mold remediation covers containment, removal and cleaning. No one can promise mold will never return, because the next leak or damp stretch brings it back.

American Fork water sources worth checking

The American Fork River runs through the city to Utah Lake, and the city says spring runoff peaks from April through June 1. It also says it is not responsible for providing flood protection to personal property. During those weeks, check basement walls and window wells after each rain, because damp areas that appear then are your problem to find.

Yard water is a second source. The Utah Department of Health and Human Services says secondary irrigation water is not treated, not monitored for germs and should only be used to water yards. If a leaking line or sprinkler head soaks the soil beside your house and water enters a wall, handle it with care and ask a professional how to proceed. Crews also work in Alpine and Lehi.
