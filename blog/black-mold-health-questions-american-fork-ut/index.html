Black Mold Questions Homeowners Ask in American Fork, UT

Plain answers on color, testing, federal limits and the building-side steps, with health questions left to a physician.

Call (385) 988-9410

Black mold is a popular name, not a diagnosis. Dark spots on a wall, ceiling or grout line can come from many kinds of mold, and no one can name the type by looking at the color. What matters for the house is the same in every case: find the moisture, dry the area and remove the growth and the materials it has damaged.

This article answers the questions we hear most. It does not make health claims and does not diagnose anything. Questions about symptoms or exposure belong to a physician, and you should take them there first if you are worried.

What people mean when they say black mold

The phrase usually describes any dark green, gray or black growth. A homeowner finds it near a shower, under a sink or on a basement wall and wants to know whether it is a special type. Color is not evidence of that, and neither is texture, smell or how fast it spread.

Treating color as a verdict leads people either to panic over dark stains or to shrug off pale growth. Both reactions miss the real issue, which is moisture.

What the EPA says about testing

The EPA says that if visible mold is present, sampling is usually unnecessary. You already know it is there, and the next step is the same either way. A test can cost time and money without changing the plan.

The EPA also says there are no federal limits for mold in buildings. A lab report with spore counts has nothing federal to be compared against, so a number alone cannot tell you a space is acceptable or not. That is why careful companies lead with the source of moisture and the extent of damage.

Health questions belong to a physician

We are a restoration company, and we will not tell you what mold does or does not do to a person. People differ, and only a physician can look at a specific case. If someone in the house has concerns, schedule that appointment now, separately from the building work.

A crew can answer questions about the structure: how wet it is, what it is made of, what has to come out and what steps keep dust from spreading to other rooms during the work. Ask for those answers in writing.

What to do about the building

  1. Stop the water

    A pipe, a roof edge, a window well or condensation is feeding the growth. Without a fix, anything you remove will be replaced by new growth.

  2. Dry it fast

    The EPA says to dry water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth.

  3. Remove what cannot dry

    Items wet for more than 24 to 48 hours that cannot be dried should be discarded, and porous materials such as ceiling tiles and carpet may have to go if moldy.

  4. Clean hard surfaces

    Scrub mold off hard surfaces with detergent and water and dry them completely.

  5. Keep records

    Take photos before and after and save receipts in case your insurer asks.

The EPA notes that areas smaller than about 10 square feet can often be cleaned by the homeowner. Larger areas, growth inside walls or anything that followed sewage or contaminated water is a job for a professional. For sewage or contaminated water, the EPA says to call a professional with experience.

When to hire a professional

Signs it is time to call

  • ✓Growth covers more than about 10 square feet or keeps returning after cleaning
  • ✓The wall or ceiling is soft, bubbling or stained from the inside
  • ✓The moisture came from sewage, a drain backup or another contaminated source
  • ✓A musty smell lingers with no visible source
  • ✓The house was built before 1978 and demolition is needed
  • ✓You cannot find or reach the moisture source

A mold remediation crew scopes the job with a moisture meter, contains the work area, removes affected materials and cleans what remains. Ask whether its crews work to ANSI/IICRC S520, the industry standard for mold remediation, fourth edition, 2024, and for ANSI/IICRC S500 on the water side. We do not claim certification, and you should ask any company to say plainly what it holds.

When mold followed a flood or a pipe break, the drying stage comes first, and water damage cleanup is the service that gets the structure dry enough for mold work to be finished. No one can promise mold will never return, because new moisture brings it back.

American Fork houses and what is in the walls

The city's historic walking tour lists downtown buildings built from 1877 to 1911. The Utah DEQ says asbestos-containing materials are still found in many older homes, including vinyl tile and sheet rock, and you cannot tell by looking. If mold work means cutting into older drywall or flooring, ask for testing of suspect materials before anyone disturbs them.

Materials found to contain asbestos must be removed by a certified asbestos contractor before being disturbed, and the EPA says contractors in homes built before 1978 must use lead-safe practices on emergency renovations. Homeowners in Pleasant Grove and Lehi should ask the same questions.
