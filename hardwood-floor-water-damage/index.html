Hardwood Floor Water Damage Repair in American Fork, UT

Wood moves when it gains or loses moisture, so a leak shows up in the boards as cupping, crowning, gaps or dark stains. What you do in the first days decides how many boards can stay.

Call (385) 988-9410

Hardwood floors can get wet from a dishwasher or sink leak, a pipe in the wall, a window or door that let in storm water, or damp air from a crawl space. A floor that looks fine on the day of the leak can lift at the edges days later.

Why boards move: moisture content

Wood is always trading moisture with the air around it. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory describes wood as swelling when it gains moisture and shrinking when it loses it. In its floor-care publication it also says a significant rise in moisture content can swell the boards enough to leave a buckled floor.

Solid and engineered wood react differently. Solid planks swell across their width and can push against each other and the walls. Engineered boards have a wood veneer over layered backing, so they often hold flatter, but water that gets into the seams can lift the top layer or loosen the glue.

What you see and what it means

Common wet-floor symptoms
What you seeWhat it often points to
Cupping: board edges higher than the centerMoisture from below, such as a damp subfloor or crawl space, or water that soaked in from the sides.
Crowning: board centers higher than the edgesOften shows after a cupped floor was sanded while still wet, or when the top dried faster than the underside.
Buckling: boards lifting off the subfloorA large amount of moisture. This usually needs prompt attention and may mean replacement.
Dark gray or black stainsWater that stayed on the wood long enough to stain the fibers or the finish.
Gaps between boardsBoards that shrank after losing moisture. See the note on dry-air gaps below.

Gaps in winter are not always a leak. When the furnace runs and indoor air dries out, many floors open slightly along the seams and close again in warmer, damper months. A gap with no cupping, no stain and no history of water is more likely seasonal. A gap beside a cupped board, a stain or a wet subfloor is a moisture question, and a meter will tell the two apart.

Safety and first-hour steps

  1. Stop the source

    Close the fixture valve or, if you cannot find the leak, the main water shutoff where the line enters the house. For a pipe in the wall, shut the main before anything else.

  2. Mind the electrical

    Do not walk through standing water near outlets, baseboard heaters or a plugged-in dishwasher. Turn off the breaker for that area if water reached a receptacle.

  3. Lift what sits on the wood

    Take up rugs, mats and furniture. Covered wood stays wet longer. Towel up loose water.

  4. Photograph the floor

    Take wide and close-up photos. For claims, the Utah Insurance Department advises photographing the damage and contacting your insurer promptly.

  5. Call before the boards change

How we dry a wood floor

We begin with readings. A pin meter or pinless meter checks the boards, the subfloor and the wall base against a dry reference area in the same house, which follows the ANSI/IICRC S500 reference for professional water damage restoration. Over a crawl space, we also check the joists and the underside.

Drying happens in a controlled way. Floor drying mats or panels draw moisture out through the surface, air movers move air across and under the boards, and dehumidifiers keep room air dry enough to accept it. The EPA remediation guide lists dehumidifiers, gentle heat and fans for wood, and it adds a plain caution: use care when applying heat to hardwood floors.

After that we wait. Boards often keep changing for days, and we do not decide what to replace until readings are stable and the floor has had time to settle. The EPA flood cleanup page mentions a moisture meter reading of 15 percent or less before refinishing. Sanding and refinishing come last, not first.

What changes the cost of hardwood floor work

  • Square footage that is wet and how far the water spread under the finish.
  • Solid or engineered wood, and whether the floor is nailed, glued or floating.
  • Days of drying needed to bring boards and subfloor to a stable reading.
  • Number of boards to replace and whether a match is available.
  • Whether the subfloor, underlayment or crawl-space framing also needs drying.
  • Whether the room must be refinished to blend repairs.

Insurance fit for wood floors

Policies differ on sudden versus gradual leaks, and flood and sewer losses are often handled separately. Ask your insurer how your loss is classified. Keep photos, moisture readings and receipts, and do not schedule permanent floor repairs until the insurer has inspected, as the Utah Insurance Department advises.

Mistakes that cost boards

  • Sanding too early. Sanding a floor that is still wet can lock in crowning or cupping and make it worse once the boards dry.
  • Covering wet floors with rugs, plastic or boxes, which holds water against the wood.
  • Using a heat gun, space heater or hair dryer to rush drying. Fast heat can crack and split boards.
  • Waiting a week for boards to flatten on their own. The EPA says mold can grow on wood that stays wet for more than 24 hours.
